How to choose the right fat separator

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

A fat separator is a simple kitchen tool that lets you pour off clear, lean juices from roasted meats, stocks, or soups while leaving the unwanted fat behind. The best choice for most home cooks is a heat-resistant glass separator with a fine-mesh strainer and a tight-sealing lid; look for a capacity that matches your typical batch size and a spout low enough to drain every last drop without tipping the container.

What is a fat separator and why do you need one?

A fat separator, sometimes called a gravy separator, is a pouring vessel with a spout that rises from the bottom of the container. Because fat floats, you pour from the bottom, leaving the surface fat behind. It’s the fastest, cleanest way to degrease pan drippings, stock, or soup without waiting for the liquid to chill or fussing with a ladle. If you roast meat often, make homemade stock, or like to skim sauce, a fat separator saves time and keeps your counter cleaner. It also helps you control how much fat stays in your food. Serious home cooks and health-conscious cooks find it indispensable.

Glass vs plastic: Which material is better?

Glass fat separators withstand high heat without warping or absorbing odors, and they let you see the fat layer clearly so you know when to stop pouring. They are heavier and can break if dropped, but most heat-resistant glass models are dishwasher-safe. Plastic separators are lighter, less likely to shatter, and often more budget-friendly. However, some plastics can retain stains or odors over time, especially if you work with strongly flavored stocks. A well-made plastic model with thick walls and a stain-resistant coating can still perform well for years. If you plan to pour very hot liquid directly from the roasting pan, glass is the safer, more durable choice. For occasional use or lighter-duty tasks, a quality plastic separator works fine.

What capacity do you need?

The right capacity depends on how much liquid you typically degrease at once. For a single roasted chicken or a small pan of drippings, a separator that holds 2 to 4 cups is plenty. For holiday turkeys, large batches of stock, or big pots of soup, look for a model that holds 4 to 6 cups. Larger separators can be awkward to handle when full. Most fat separators have measurement markings on the side, which help you gauge how much liquid you have left. If you often work with both small and large volumes, consider owning two sizes or choose a medium capacity, around 4 cups, as a versatile middle ground.

Spout design and strainer: What to look for?

The spout shape matters more than you might think. A low spout that sits very close to the bottom allows you to drain almost all of the lean liquid without tipping the separator. Some models have a curved spout that pours steadily without drips. A rotating spout that can switch from pouring to sealing is a nice convenience, but make sure the seal is tight to avoid leaks when you shake or carry the separator. A built-in strainer is a major time-saver. It catches small bits of meat, herbs, or sediment that would otherwise end up in your sauce. The best strainers are fine-mesh, removable, and dishwasher-safe. If you already own a fine-mesh strainer, you can live without one built-in, but it’s far more convenient to have it integrated.

Ease of cleaning and dishwasher safety

Cleaning a fat separator can be tricky if it has narrow openings or non-removable parts. Look for a model with a wide mouth that lets you scrub the inside easily. A removable strainer and a fully detachable lid make cleaning much simpler. Most glass separators are top-rack dishwasher-safe, but check that the lid and strainer are rated for the dishwasher too, especially if they have plastic components. For plastic models, avoid abrasive scouring pads that scratch the surface. Scratches can harbor bacteria and make the separator look worn. A few minutes of rinsing and a gentle scrub with a bottle brush is usually enough for everyday use. If you degrease particularly fatty drippings, a quick rinse with hot water and a drop of dish soap right after use prevents stuck-on residue.

What do owners say about the top brands?

The most consistently praised fat separators in owner reviews and expert roundups are from OXO, Cuisinart, and Griptite. OXO’s model is often noted for its comfortable, non-slip handle and tight-sealing lid that prevents spills. Cuisinart’s version features a built-in strainer and a durable glass body that owners say holds up well to frequent dishwasher cycles. Griptite’s separator is a budget-friendly glass option with a straightforward design that users find easy to pour from without dripping. A few negatives come up as well: some plastic separators can crack if exposed to very high heat, and certain lids don’t seal well after repeated use. Reading current owner feedback on retail sites will help you decide which model fits your kitchen habits best.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use a fat separator for hot liquids right out of the oven?

Yes, but only if the separator is made of heat-resistant glass (like borosilicate) or heat-safe plastic rated for boiling liquids. Pouring extremely hot drippings into a cold glass separator can cause thermal shock and cracking, so let the liquid cool for a minute or two first.

Do I need a built-in strainer, or can I use my own?

A built-in strainer is convenient but not essential. If you already own a fine-mesh strainer, you can hold it over the separator and pour through it. The built-in version saves a step and cuts down on cleanup, but it’s a nice-to-have rather than a must.

How do I clean a fat separator thoroughly?

Rinse it immediately after use with hot water to wash away residual fat. For stuck-on grease, use a bottle brush with a little dish soap. Most glass separators and their lids are top-rack dishwasher-safe. Avoid putting plastic separators on the bottom rack unless the manufacturer says it’s safe.

Will a fat separator work for small amounts of pan drippings?

It works, but small drippings (less than a cup) can be tricky to pour without losing a lot of liquid. For tiny amounts, a refrigerator chilling method or a spoon skimming might be easier. A separator with a capacity of 2 cups or more is better suited for at least 1 cup of liquid.

Can I separate fat from soups and stocks, not just pan drippings?

Absolutely. A fat separator works for any liquid where fat floats to the top, stock, broth, soup, and even sauces. Just make sure you strain out solid ingredients (like vegetables or meat bits) first, then pour the liquid into the separator.

Is glass or plastic better for dishwasher cleaning?

Glass generally holds up better to hot dishwasher cycles and doesn’t scratch or warp. Many plastic separators are also dishwasher-safe, but they may degrade faster over time. If you rely heavily on the dishwasher, a glass model with a stainless-steel strainer is the most durable option.

In shortChoosing a fat separator comes down to a few simple trade-offs: glass for durability and heat tolerance, plastic for lightness and lower cost; a built-in strainer for convenience, and a capacity that matches your cooking volume. Look for a model with a low spout, a tight lid, and dishwasher-safe parts. A good fat separator will pay for itself in saved time and cleaner sauces the first time you use it on a holiday roast.
